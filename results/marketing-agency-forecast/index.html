The situation

The agency runs two service models: direct client work and white label work for other agencies. Two owners, both deep in delivery.

At the end of 2025 the honest assessment was that a very busy year had left "zero margin for company growth."

The owners needed to step out of daily operations for the company to become more valuable. No amount of working harder solves that.

The tension most case studies leave out

A leaner team made cash flow more predictable. It also made bandwidth thinner and the work heavier. As one owner put it, having almost no employees looks good on paper and increases the workload. Both were true at once.

What we did

Built the forecast from the bottom up

Not a growth rate applied to last year. Recurring revenue split by service model, each model split into small and large accounts by customer count and average price. Project revenue forecast separately.

Why the structure matters more than the number

It makes the forecast arguable. When the owners disagreed with an assumption, they could point at the assumption instead of at the total.

Modelled losing the largest account

One large direct account had a new decision maker shopping for something cheaper. Rather than note the risk and move on, we modelled the year with that revenue gone. If it happens, the plan exists. If it doesn't, the downside case cost an afternoon.

Re-scoped project revenue to the months it closes

The prior year's project total was inflated by a large pass-through expense, which made the base look better than it was. We cut the projection and squeezed project work into a March-to-October window.

Forecasting it evenly across twelve months had been building a miss into every Q1.

Picked the conservative acquisition assumption

The owners were aiming for a net new direct customer every month once the new staffing structure was in.

We modelled net one per quarter to start, ramping to one a month from Q2, because January and February are reliably slower. Two new small white label customers a quarter, with a marketing spend line alongside.

Split cost of goods sold by service model

Direct and white label work have different economics, and blended COGS hides which one carries the business. Splitting it made each model's real margin visible.

Reforecast when the facts changed

The forecast was rebuilt around client cancellations and a compensation change. We benchmarked a new account manager role across pay bands before anyone posted it.

We also pulled a vendor and software expense list for line-by-line review. Least glamorous item on this page, one of the most useful.

Where it landed

Q1, against Q1 last year

Revenue roughly level, with slightly more invoicing and meaningfully better profitability. Three months in a row of consistent operating profit and positive cash profit. The rolling average share of revenue going to people came down about 3 points.

The forecast itself is the quieter result. Plotted against actuals and against the prior year, all three lines came in nearly identical for the quarter. A forecast that holds for a quarter is one you can make decisions against.

The line that stuck with us came while we were sizing a future support hire against the model, and it was not about the model.

At the end of that same session: "this was wildly helpful." The other owner made the more precise point. They would have got to splitting the two service models eventually, he said, but seeing the numbers "makes it very tangible for us."

That is the argument for a bottom-up forecast. It rarely tells you something you could never have worked out. It makes the thing you half-suspected concrete enough to act on this quarter instead of next year.

Questions

The ones people actually ask on the first call

Is flat revenue a good outcome?

It is when profitability improves and the forecast holds. Revenue came in level year over year while operating profit stayed consistent for three months, which means the same top line produced more money and did it predictably.

What makes a bottom-up forecast better than a growth rate?

You can argue with it. Splitting recurring revenue by service model and then by account size means a disagreement lands on a specific assumption, like how many new accounts a quarter, instead of on a total you can't interrogate.

Why model losing your biggest client?

Because modelling it costs an afternoon and being surprised by it costs a year. Here a large account had a new decision maker looking at cheaper options, so the year was modelled with that revenue removed.

Why does project revenue get forecast separately?

Because it does not arrive evenly. Spreading project work across twelve months builds a miss into every slow quarter. Here it was re-scoped into a March-to-October window, which is when that work closes.

How much of this is the forecast and how much is the cost cuts?

Both, and they are hard to separate. Bringing people cost down about 3 points produced the margin. The forecast is what made the owners willing to make those calls, because they could see what each one did to the year first.
