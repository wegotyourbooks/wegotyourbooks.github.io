The situation

Project work is a harder reporting problem than a retainer shop. Revenue arrives in lumps. Delivery cost is hours, spread across contractors. Whether a given engagement made money is a real question.

Before

  • Books kept in-house, then handed to someone else
  • Emails going days without a reply
  • Reporting arriving on no rhythm you could plan around

What they asked for

  • Weekly reporting they could count on
  • A forecast far enough ahead to see a gap coming
  • To stop wondering whether something had been forgotten

We started in January 2025 and took the books over in February.

What we built

A weekly income and expense report

Income, cost of goods sold, expenses and net income across four discrete weeks, with a four-week total and average beside three trailing monthly columns. Underneath, 15 itemised expense lines.

Why four weeks and not one

A single week in a project business is noise. Four weeks with a running average is a signal.

A weekly receivables snapshot, and the rule for acting on it

A full aging report runs once a week. A simplified version goes to the person who owns follow-up: which customers are open, the invoice numbers, the amounts, and days past due.

Then the written part, which is what makes it work:

  1. 1

    10 days past due triggers a reminder

    A professional email, sent by their team.

  2. 2

    Every attempt gets logged

    So the log shows who was chased and when.

  3. 3

    Non-responses and disputes escalate

    To the owner, and to us.

  4. 4

    A Loom explains how to read the report

    So the process survives whoever is running it this quarter.

Who chases the money

Not us. Receivables management is excluded from every agreement we write, deliberately. We build the report that says who is late and by how much. Their team sends the emails, because a bookkeeper inside your client relationships creates more friction than it removes.

Per-project margin, because they asked for it

In August 2025 the studio told us the single profitability number on the dashboard was "too vague." Fair. It answered whether the business made money and said nothing about which work made it.

They wanted margin per project after contractor hours, rates and tax, and margin per contractor. The reason was uncomfortable: a contractor billed as full-time was working roughly half those hours at the same pay. Nothing in the reporting would have surfaced it.

How the margin number gets built

Contractors log hours in Clockify. That feeds QuickBooks against the right project and customer. Margin comes from measured time, not estimates.

The report carries hours, time cost, fixed cost, invoiced amount, margin and margin percent for every engagement, off 3,736 hours of logged contractor time.

A second grid shows margin by client by month, so an engagement going bad shows up in the month it turns rather than at year end.

A monthly performance report over the top

Revenue against target and rolling average, operating margin, break-even and margin of safety, top ten expense accounts, a three-month expense comparison, operating cash flow, and a combined cash and receivables chart. The margin series runs back several years, so the current month has context.

Questions

The ones people actually ask on the first call

How do you cut receivable days from 18 to 5?

A weekly aging snapshot plus a written rule about when to act. Any invoice 10 or more days past due triggers a reminder email, every attempt is logged, and non-responses escalate. The reporting is ours and the follow-up is their team.

Why does per-project margin need tracked hours?

Delivery cost in a project business is time, and estimated time is a guess. Building margin from time entries is what separates your best engagements from your worst.

Who sends the reminder emails on a late invoice?

Their team, never us. Receivables management is excluded from every agreement we write. We generate the aging report and the snapshot that says who is late and by how much, and a Loom explains how to read it.

Do the reports only surface good news?

No. Anything moving the wrong way sits in the same headline block as the things moving the right way, not in a footnote. A report that only shows wins stops getting read.

How long did the receivables fix take to show up?

One month. The weekly snapshot and the follow-up rule went live together, and receivable days came down 13 days over the next cycle.
